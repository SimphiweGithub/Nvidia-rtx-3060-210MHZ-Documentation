# Experimental slope override: results and limitations

On the tested Dell RTX 3060 Laptop GPU, modern drivers originally reported a fixed, implausible board-power value around 752 W and the core remained near 210 MHz without the previously used +1000 MHz offset. A driver-loaded image that changes power-monitor channel scaling and related values produced a displayed 6.1 W board reading and much lower displayed channel readings. These are altered readings, not measurements of actual power. The physical source of the original bad readings and the exact boost-policy pathway are not established.

The image was loaded through a Windows driver registry override, not by flashing the GPU firmware. The device-specific [switch](../../tools/GPU-Slope-Switch.cmd) stages or removes the override and restarts the GPU device. Enable and Disable were tested with kernel debugging disabled; readback matched the candidate after enabling and the previously loaded image after disabling. The user subsequently reported that the workaround also works with Secure Boot enabled. The switch and image are specific to this machine and driver setup, not a general fix for other GPUs. Device restart can interrupt graphics applications or fail; recovery may require a Windows restart or Safe Mode.

During GPU-Z's render test the GPU was observed at 1935 MHz core and 1750.2 MHz memory with the modified image; another test with a +300 MHz memory offset reported approximately 7300 MHz in Afterburner. A device restart during an earlier comparison was followed by memory reaching its normal performance state. These observations demonstrate boost in those tests, not stability across all games or sustained workloads. Actual GPU power and current remain unknown: the modified feedback may affect protections, and a clock/voltage curve or GPU temperature reading does not independently verify a board power limit.

Afterburner profile 4 was later adjusted to target 1900 MHz at 825 mV, with a flat curve above that point and a +300 MHz memory offset. Under a short render test, the recorded voltage was approximately 800 mV and core clock approximately 1740–1747 MHz; why the higher point was not selected remains unresolved. A user-applied temporary 825 mV lock was followed by an idle reading of 800 mV and 1747 MHz; the user later reported the lock was gone and believed it had not applied. Neither the intended lock nor its removal was independently verified in the UI. Previous higher-frequency settings crashed or nearly crashed, and the 1900 MHz target has not been demonstrated stable.

Raw kernel event trace files and Python cache files are excluded from the published changes; the local copies remain available. The [kernel investigation](live-kernel-debugger-2026-11-08.md) and captured telemetry provide further detail.
